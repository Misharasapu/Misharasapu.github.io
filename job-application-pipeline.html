Job application pipeline

A tool I built for my own job search that turns a pasted job description into a fit assessment, a tailored one-page LaTeX CV and a logged application, with a human decision at every step and one self-correcting loop that keeps the CV to exactly one page.

Problem

Applying to technical roles is mostly mechanical work wrapped around a few judgement calls: read the description, decide whether it is worth applying, reframe your experience, rewrite a CV that still fits on one page, check whether the company can sponsor a visa and record the application. The judgement calls are small and the mechanical work is large, which is what makes it worth automating while keeping a person on the decisions.

Approach

The system is two independent halves that share only the Google Sheets tracker. The application pipeline runs when I find a role and chains five steps in a fixed order, and the sourcing agent runs in the background when the laptop wakes and brings a filtered digest of new roles.

I used two model tiers on purpose, because the tasks are different: Claude Sonnet for the fit card, the CV and the cover letter, where output quality is the whole point, and Claude Haiku for the sponsor check, which is just confirming which of up to twenty fuzzy-matched company names is the right one. The fit card's schema is enforced by the API's structured output, so a malformed response is stopped at the source instead of caught later.

It is a prompt-chaining pipeline with one agentic loop, and I describe it that way on purpose: the control flow is ordinary Python and I stay in the loop at every decision that matters, because a wrong autonomous decision here means a bad application sent under my name.

Key decision: measure the PDF, not the model's opinion of it

A model asked for a one-page CV routinely produces a page and a half or three quarters of a page. So the CV step compiles the PDF, counts the pages and measures how full page one is from the rendered glyph positions, then trims or expands in whichever direction is wrong, for at most three corrections, and keeps the best valid page seen so far so the loop can never finish on something worse than it already had.

Results

Giving the first draft a length budget, measured from 100 accepted CVs and stated as point figures rather than ranges, cut the mean first-draft overflow from 7.2 lines to 0.4 across three test job descriptions, so most remaining misses need only one small trim. Replacing the old page-fill reader with a glyph-based one brought the worst-case error down to 0.08 points against an independent pixel-rendering check.

Prompt caching with a one-hour lifetime cut system-prompt input cost by roughly 58% over a four-application session. That figure was measured before I split the prompt into one file per agent, so it should be read as the pre-split number.

The target is under eight minutes per application, and I have not measured it yet, so it stays labelled as a target.

Limitations and next steps

  • Time per application is a target, not a measurement. Timing a defined set of applications is the next thing to do.
  • Sourcing covers Adzuna only, and archetype labels and seniority filters are keyword-based, so an unusual job title can be mislabelled.
  • The sponsor check confirms that a company is on the register, not that it will sponsor this particular role, and the output says so.
  • The page-fill measure uses the top and bottom rendered lines, so it cannot tell a page whose last line is one word from a full one.
  • It only runs on macOS, because it relies on launchd, sleepwatcher and macOS notifications.
